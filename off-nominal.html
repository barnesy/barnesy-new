Off Nominal · Pair-coded with Claude

A weekend UAP browser that grew into a correlation engine

It started as 222 declassified UAP files made browsable over a weekend. Then I kept pulling the thread — adding nuclear tests, reactor incidents, physics milestones, and the CIA's Stargate archive until it held 2,737 records. The hard part wasn't connecting them. It was building the honesty layer that tells you when a connection is real and when you're just seeing faces in the clouds. Off Nominal is NASA's term for anything that doesn't behave as expected; the name fit.

Why I started

The Department of War's PURSUE initiative (Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters) is a rolling declassification project. The first two drops — May 8 and May 22, 2026 — put 222 records online, contributed by DoW, the FBI, NASA, ODNI, DOE, the State Department, and the CIA.

The official UX at war.gov/ufo is a paginated table behind heavy bot protection. Useful if you know the exact file you want; almost useless if you're asking "where and when did this stuff actually happen?"

That's the question I wanted to answer. A timeline of UAP incidents across 80 years, a globe of where they happened, a side panel for the case detail, and one set of filters that drives all three. Build it, browse it, link every dot back to the official .gov source. Host nothing of theirs.

That was the weekend. What you'll read below is what happened when I didn't stop — because once UAP cases sat on a timeline next to nuclear tests, the obvious next question was whether the famous "UFOs cluster around atomic tests" claim actually holds up. Answering that honestly turned a browser into something else entirely.

12 seconds of the actual app. Brush a date range on the timeline. The map filters to the same period — the modern UAP wave is concentrated in CENTCOM and the Western US. Click a marker, pick a case, the detail panel slides in.

The build, briefly

Pair-coded with Claude. Vite, React, TypeScript, MUI. d3-scale for the timeline, d3-geo + TopoJSON for the map, MapLibre for the 3D nuclear-blast view I added later. The whole thing is a static site — no servers, no databases, no paid APIs, no LLM at runtime. Every heavy computation runs once at build time and ships as plain JSON; the browser just renders it.

The first surprise was how easy the data was to get: the entire DoW release is a 297 KB CSV. The "interactive table" on the official site is jQuery DataTables reading that one file. The bigger surprise came later — that the same build-time-JSON pattern would scale all the way up to a 2,737-record statistical engine without ever needing a backend.

Click any dot. The detail panel slides in, the map and timeline stay live. The Open source PDF button takes you to the official media.defense.gov file.

What I learned

The legal/ethical posture matters more than the engineering.

Federal works are public domain by statute (17 USC §105). PURSUE was released for the public; civic reuse is the explicit intent. But war.gov is Akamai-protected and blocks even /robots.txt from a plain curl. Getting through requires Playwright with a real-Chrome channel and a couple of stealth flags — which crosses from "polite scraper" into "actively evading their bot detection."

Talked it through with the user, made the call: pull the one index CSV once, never re-host any source material, link every case back to war.gov or media.defense.gov by URL. The repo is about 5 KB of JSON and code. Anything you want to read, you read on the government's servers.

Then it grew

Putting UAP cases on a timeline next to nuclear tests is irresistible — it's the oldest claim in the field. So I added the data to actually look: 2,057 nuclear weapons tests (SIPRI), nuclear accidents and reactor incidents, a curated set of nuclear-physics milestones (1896 onward), the CIA Stargate remote-viewing archive (337 documents), and the key published works the files cite. Seven datasets, 2,737 records, one shared timeline and map.

Datasets sitting side by side isn't insight, though — it's just more dots. The unlock was an entity network: a curated registry of the people and places that recur across the corpus, so the same figure or location stitches records together regardless of which dataset they live in. Harold Puthoff connects his SRI Stargate files to the papers he authored. "New Mexico" links a 1948 UAP sighting at Sandia to the Trinity test and the physics milestones around it. Click any name or place and you see everywhere it appears, across all seven datasets at once.

All of it built the same way as the original weekend: extracted once at build time, shipped as static JSON, rendered in the browser. The network, the search index, the statistics — none of it needs a server. It compounds.

The honesty layer

Here's the trap with a tool like this: it will always find connections. Two events near each other in time and space, a shared keyword, a name that appears twice — pattern-matching never comes up empty. A UFO-conspiracy generator is a weekend project. The interesting, harder thing is a tool that tells you when a pattern is nothing.

So the centerpiece isn't the connections — it's the skepticism. Three layers, all computed, all deterministic, no LLM deciding anything:

  • Monte Carlo p-values. For each UAP case, how often would a random date land as close to a nuclear test as the real one? If tests fired weekly for decades, "near a test" is the default, not a signal. The result is the whole project in one number: of 180 UAP cases tested, 172 are statistically coincidental and only 2 are significant. The famous UFO–nuclear correlation is, mostly, the dense test calendar.
  • An adversarial skeptic. Ask the tool to test any connection and it argues against it — grounded in the actual numbers — then renders a verdict: "Survives scrutiny," "Plausible, unproven," or "Likely coincidence," scored 0–100. It weights hard documentary links (a shared named person) above the soft signals coincidence can fake (timing, shared vocabulary).
  • Show your work. Every correlation expands into the evidence behind it — shared people, places, distance, time gap, overlapping terms, significance — each with a strength bar. Nothing is asserted; everything is shown, so you can judge it instead of trusting it.

That principle even survives into the export: collect a set of records and the tool assembles a deterministic investigation brief — print-to-PDF, no AI prose — that reports which connections held up and which it dismissed as chance. The skepticism travels with the document.

"Of 180 UAP cases tested against the nuclear-test calendar, 172 are coincidental and 2 are significant. The tool says so out loud."

What's in there now

Timeline, world map & 3D blast view

The original swimlane timeline and Equal Earth world map, now spanning all seven datasets with a shared, lifted-up filter set — datasets, agencies, yield, altitude, era. Brush a date range and every view follows. Nuclear tests also open a physically-modeled 3D detonation view (MapLibre) at true scale.

The entity network

People, places, and publications as first-class objects. A detail panel lists the figures and locations a record touches; click one for a cross-dataset dossier of everywhere it appears. A corpus-wide "top connectors" feed surfaces the names and places that bridge the most datasets.

The Connections Explorer & corpus dashboard

A browser over the 200 strongest scored cross-document links — filter by connection type, sort by the skeptic's verdict, expand any link to its evidence, export to Markdown or JSON. An overview tab visualizes the p-value distribution as a histogram: the anti-pattern-matching argument, made into a chart.

Investigations & an MCP server

Collect records into a working set, generate the deterministic brief, and share it via a URL hash — no backend, no account. And because the analytics are just functions over static JSON, the same engine is exposed as a local Model Context Protocol server: an AI agent can investigate the corpus through tools that lead with the skeptic's verdict, so it can't narrate a coincidence as a finding either.

Why this one matters to me

Off Nominal is the clearest example I have of what designing with an AI partner can become when you don't stop at the first working version. A weekend browser, iterated over many sessions, turned into a genuine research instrument — and the most important design decision in all of it wasn't visual. It was choosing to make the tool skeptical of itself: to spend the engineering on the layer that says "this is probably nothing," in a subject that runs entirely on the opposite instinct.

It also stayed honest to its constraints the whole way: zero servers, zero paid APIs, no model running at inference time — a 2,737-record statistical engine that's still just a static site, hosting nothing of anyone else's. Every record links out to its official home. The whole archive is one rebuild away from current.
