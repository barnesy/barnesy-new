Off Nominal · A dataviz rabbit hole

Off Nominal Dataviz

It started innocently: 222 declassified UFO files, dropped onto a timeline and a map over a weekend. Then I had the obvious thought — "what if I plotted every nuclear test on top?" — and could not stop. Reactor meltdowns, physics milestones, the CIA's psychic-spy program… 2,737 records later, I had to build a little statistics machine just to stop myself from connecting dots that aren't there. Off Nominal is NASA-speak for "this isn't behaving as expected." Felt about right.

UFO–nuke "links" that survived the math

It started as a table I couldn't read

In May 2026 the Department of War actually started unsealing its UFO files (real program, real acronym: PURSUE). The first drops put 222 records online — from the DoW, FBI, NASA, the CIA, and friends. The catch: the official site is a paginated table. Great if you already know the file you want. Useless for the only question I actually had, which was "okay, but where and when did this stuff happen?"

So I built the thing I wanted: a timeline of 80 years of sightings, a globe of where they landed, and a side panel for the juicy details — all driven by one set of filters. A weekend of pair-coding with Claude and I had a browsable UFO map. Cute. Done. I should have stopped there.

I did not stop there. Because the second those UFO dots sat on a timeline, my brain went straight to the oldest bar-stool theory in the genre: don't UFOs always show up near nuclear stuff? There was only one way to find out — drop every nuclear test on top and look.

The real thing, in 18 seconds: brush a date range, pop open the Connections Explorer, and meet the coincidence histogram — 172 of 180 "UFOs near nukes" links are just the busy bomb calendar. Then a top connection unfolds to show its receipts.

The boring-but-load-bearing bits

It's all a static site — no servers, no database, no API I have to pay for, no AI running while you use it. Vite, React, TypeScript, d3 for the charts, MapLibre for the nuclear-blast view I absolutely did not need to add but did anyway. Anything expensive (the stats, the search index, the connection graph) gets computed once when I build the site and shipped as plain JSON. Your browser just draws it.

Funniest discovery of the whole project: that entire government "interactive database" of UFO files? It's a single 297 KB spreadsheet a bit of jQuery is reading. One download and the whole archive is on my laptop. And out of respect (and copyright sanity), the site re-hosts none of the source material — every one of the 2,737 dots is just a link back to its official home on war.gov, the CIA reading room, Wikipedia, SIPRI. Click a dot, read it on their servers, not mine.

Click a dot, get the actual case — a 1973 CIA report of a "luminous bright green" object over the USSR, in this case. The "Open source" button sends you to the real government file.

…and then I just kept feeding it

One nuclear dataset is never enough. So in went 2,057 nuclear tests, the reactor meltdowns and "broken arrow" accidents, a greatest-hits list of physics milestones back to 1896, and — because why not — the CIA's actual Stargate psychic-spy archive (337 documents of the US government taking telepathy very seriously). Seven datasets, 2,737 dots, all sharing one timeline and map.

But a pile of dots from different files is just… a bigger pile. The fun part was teaching them to talk to each other — a network of the people and places that keep showing up. Now the same physicist links his psychic-research files to the papers he wrote; "New Mexico" quietly connects a 1948 UFO flap at Sandia to the Trinity test and the bomb-makers down the road. Click any name or place and watch it light up everywhere it appears, across all seven datasets at once. This is the part that feels like pulling a thread and watching the whole sweater move.

So I built a machine to ruin my own conspiracy

Here's the trap, and it's a fun one: a tool like this will connect anything. Two events near each other, a shared word, a name that pops up twice — it will always find something. Point it at UFOs and bombs and it'll hand you a "smoking gun" every single time. Which makes it worthless, because a tool that's impressed by everything is impressed by nothing.

The actually-interesting build wasn't the connection-finder. It was the buzzkill standing next to it. Three layers of professional skeptic, all just math, no AI vibes:

  • A coincidence detector. For each UFO sighting it asks: if I threw a dart at a random date, how often would it land this close to a nuclear test anyway? Turns out the bomb calendar was packed — tests went off constantly for decades — so "near a test" is just… Tuesday. The whole punchline in one number: of 180 sightings checked, 172 are pure coincidence and only 2 are genuinely weird. The famous UFO–nuke correlation is mostly the US testing a lot of nukes.
  • A built-in skeptic you can argue with. Pick any two records, hit "test this connection," and the tool argues against you — with the actual numbers — then drops a verdict: "Survives scrutiny," "Plausible," or the devastating "Likely coincidence," scored out of 100. A shared named person counts for a lot; "they both happened in the 1960s" counts for basically nothing.
  • Receipts for everything. Every claimed link expands to show its work — who, where, how far apart, how long apart, which words overlap — each with a little strength bar. Nothing is asserted at you. It's all shown, so you can roll your eyes at it yourself.

Best part: the skepticism comes with you. Star a few records and the tool prints a tidy investigation brief (PDF, no AI-written fluff) that lists which connections held up and which it threw out as nonsense. You can take your debunking to go.

"172 of 180. The big spooky UFO–nuclear pattern is, mathematically, the sound of a very busy bomb-testing schedule. The tool says so, out loud, to my face."

Stuff you can actually click

Timeline + map + a gratuitous 3D mushroom cloud

The original timeline and world map, now holding all seven datasets with one shared set of filters. Drag a date range and everything follows. And because I couldn't help myself, every nuclear test opens a to-scale 3D detonation view — fully unnecessary, extremely fun.

The "everything is connected" network

Click any person or place and watch it light up across every dataset at once — the part that makes you feel like a detective with a corkboard and red string. The home screen even ranks the biggest "connectors": the names and spots that quietly tie the most files together.

The Connections Explorer (with built-in skeptic)

A leaderboard of the 200 strongest cross-document links, each stamped with the skeptic's verdict. Sort the most-likely-real to the top, expand any one for its receipts, export the lot to Markdown or JSON. The overview tab is my favorite chart in the whole thing: the coincidence histogram, where the "UFOs love nukes" theory goes to quietly deflate.

Save it, share it, or let a robot poke at it

Star a set of records, generate the brief, and share the whole investigation as a link — no login, no backend, just a big URL. And since it's all just functions over static files, I wrapped the same engine as a little server an AI assistant can query directly — politely forced to lead with the skeptic's verdict, so even the robot can't oversell a coincidence.

The most fun I've had being wrong

I went in half-hoping to find the pattern. The data politely declined. And honestly? That's the part I'm proudest of — the most interesting design call in the whole project wasn't a color or a layout, it was deciding to build the thing that argues back. In a subject that runs entirely on "everything is connected, man," I spent the real effort on the little voice that says "…is it, though?"

It's also just a nice reminder of how far a weekend toy can go when you keep pulling the thread with a good pair-programming partner — a 2,737-record stats engine that's still, somehow, just a static webpage. No servers, nothing to pay for, hosting none of the actual files. Click any dot, read the real thing on the government's own site. The whole archive is one rebuild away from current — so when they unseal Release 03, the aliens are welcome back any time.

Go find a pattern (the skeptic will be waiting)

Bonus points if you find one of the 2 sightings that actually survives. The real government files are one click further if you want to read the source for yourself.
